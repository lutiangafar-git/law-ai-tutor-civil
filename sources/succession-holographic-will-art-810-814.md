# Source text
Art. 810. A person may execute a holographic will which must be entirely written, dated, and signed by the hand of the testator himself. It is subject to no other form, and may be made in or out of the Philippines, and need not be witnessed.

Art. 814. In case of any insertion, cancellation, erasure or alteration in a holographic will, the testator must authenticate the same by his full signature.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- A holographic will must be entirely written, dated, and signed by the hand of the testator; it needs no witnesses (Art. 810).
- Any insertion, cancellation, erasure or alteration in a holographic will must be authenticated by the testator's full signature (Art. 814).
- As a rule, an unauthenticated alteration does not invalidate the whole will; at most, only the particular words altered are affected (Kalaw v. Relova, G.R. No. L-40207, September 28, 1984).
- Exception: where the will has only one substantial disposition and that disposition is altered without authentication, the will is void. The original wording cannot be given effect because the testator changed it, and the change cannot be given effect because it was not authenticated (Kalaw v. Relova).

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: Hilda's holographic will, entirely written, dated and signed by her, gives her car to Ivan and her house to Joy. Later, Hilda crossed out "Ivan" and wrote "Karl" beside it, but did not sign the change. Is the whole will void?
Model answer: No. The will has more than one disposition, so under the general rule an unauthenticated alteration does not invalidate the will as a whole; at most, only the altered words are affected. The gift of the house to Joy remains valid. The change naming Karl cannot be given effect because Hilda did not authenticate it with her full signature, as Art. 814 requires.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (2012 Bar Examination, Civil Law, Question VII.a) Natividad's holographic will, which had only one (1) substantial provision, as first written, named Rosa as her sole heir. However, when Gregorio presented it for probate, it already contained an alteration, naming Gregorio, instead of Rosa, as sole heir, but without authentication by Natividad's signature. Rosa opposes the probate alleging such lack of proper authentication. She claims that the unaltered form of the will should be given effect. Whose claim should be granted? Explain.
Model answer:
- Answer: Neither claim should be granted. The holographic will is void.
- Legal basis: Under Art. 814 of the Civil Code, any alteration in a holographic will must be authenticated by the testator's full signature. In Kalaw v. Relova (G.R. No. L-40207, September 28, 1984), the Court held that where a holographic will has only one substantial provision and it is altered without the required authentication, the will is void.
- Application: The will's only substantial provision, the institution of the sole heir, was changed from Rosa to Gregorio without Natividad's full signature. Gregorio cannot rely on the change because it was not authenticated. Rosa cannot rely on the original wording because Natividad evidently changed her mind about it. With no other provision left, nothing in the will can be given effect.
- Conclusion: The will cannot be probated as written or as altered, so neither Rosa's nor Gregorio's claim should be granted.
