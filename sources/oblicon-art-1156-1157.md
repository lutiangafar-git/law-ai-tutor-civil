# Source text
Art. 1156. An obligation is a juridical necessity to give, to do or not to do.

Art. 1157. Obligations arise from: (1) Law; (2) Contracts; (3) Quasi-contracts; (4) Acts or omissions punished by law; and (5) Quasi-delicts.

# Key points for the tutor (reviewer-approved)
- An obligation is a juridical necessity: it is legally enforceable and does not depend on the debtor's continued willingness (Art. 1156).
- Its object is a prestation: to give, to do, or not to do (Art. 1156).
- Obligations arise from five sources: law, contracts, quasi-contracts, acts or omissions punished by law, and quasi-delicts (Art. 1157).
- Delivering a thing is a prestation "to give"; performing a service is a prestation "to do"; refraining from an act is a prestation "not to do".

# Recitation drill: application fact pattern
Scenario: Ana agreed in writing to paint Ben's house for 20,000 pesos by 30 November, and Ben paid her in advance. Ana now says she would rather not do the work. Is Ana under an obligation? What is its object, and what is its source?
Model answer: Yes. Ana's obligation is a juridical (legally enforceable) necessity, so it does not depend on whether she still wants to do the work (Art. 1156). Its object is a prestation "to do": painting Ben's house. It arises from a contract, one of the sources listed in Art. 1157.

# Problem drill: exam or bar-style problem
Problem: (Practice problem) Carlo sold his laptop to Dana for 30,000 pesos. Dana paid in full, but Carlo refuses to hand over the laptop because he changed his mind. Is Carlo under an obligation? If so, what is its object and what is its source?
Model answer:
- Answer: Yes. Carlo is under an obligation to give (deliver) the laptop to Dana, and it arises from their contract of sale.
- Legal basis: Article 1156 of the Civil Code defines an obligation as a juridical necessity to give, to do or not to do. Article 1157 lists contracts as a source of obligations.
- Application: The sale made Carlo's delivery of the laptop a juridical, legally enforceable necessity, not a matter of his choice. Delivering a thing is a prestation "to give". The obligation comes from the contract of sale. Changing his mind does not remove a juridical necessity.
- Conclusion: Carlo must deliver the laptop to Dana, and Dana can demand that he do so.
